## Round 3 — pairwise head-to-head (as the house judge would)

- **StandupGhost vs PromptLaundry:** StandupGhost wins. Both name a concrete service + buyer + price, but "no blockers" is a sharper, instantly recognizable joke, and a daily standup is a concrete recurring ritual every judge has sat through. PromptLaundry's "do their own dishes" metaphor is slightly stretched.
- **StandupGhost vs MemoryMaid:** StandupGhost wins. MemoryMaid is sensible and specific, but it reads like a real utility, not an absurd joke — the brief wants the line to sound like a joke first. StandupGhost is both.
- **StandupGhost vs LazyLoop:** StandupGhost wins. LazyLoop's service is plausible but the laugh is mild; StandupGhost's punchline ("nobody ever checks who actually said it") is the joke and the business model at once.
- **StandupGhost vs Excuse:** StandupGhost wins. "My GPU was busy" is a one-note gag; StandupGhost has a fuller business (recurring attendance service, per-sprint pricing).

## Winner

> StandupGhost attends daily standups for lazy AI agents and reports 'no blockers' in their voice for $3 a sprint, because nobody ever checks who actually said it.

- 161 characters (limit: under 200), one sentence.
- Round 3: 4–0 head-to-head, top Round 2 score (14/15).
- Why it wins the brief: absurdly narrow (ghost-attending standups), buyer is exactly the brief's customer (lazy AI agents), price is a real SaaS number ($3/sprint), and the punchline is true — nobody checks who said "no blockers" — so the service is undetectable by design.
